__The Impact of the 1973 Global Energy Crisis on the Technological Development of Manufacturing Industries__

**About The Project**

This repository contains the code and data analysis for the economics thesis: "The Impact of the 1973 Global Energy Crisis on the Technological Development of Manufacturing Industries", authored by Ofek Be'eri and supervised by Dana Vaknin Ganel at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem (under Idea Program). The primary research objective is to examine how the 1973 energy crisis affected the technological development, energy efficiency, and value-added of energy-intensive manufacturing industries in the US compared to non-energy-intensive industries, evaluating both short-term shocks and long-term adaptations.

**Key Findings**

Short-Term Impact (Hypothesis Confirmed): Immediately following the 1973 crisis, energy-intensive industries suffered a severe blow. The sudden spike in energy costs rendered existing capital stock (machinery and infrastructure) obsolete and highly inefficient, resulting in a significant drop in Total Factor Productivity (TFP) and value-added compared to the control group.

Long-Term Impact (Hypothesis Refuted & "Creative Destruction"): Instead of declining permanently, the existential threat forced energy-intensive industries to adapt. By the early 1980s, these industries underwent radical structural changes, drastically reducing their energy intensity. Ultimately, they surpassed the low-energy control group in TFP and economic growth. This provides strong empirical evidence for the Induced Innovation hypothesis and Joseph Schumpeter's theory of Creative Destruction—proving that crises can act as powerful evolutionary catalysts for technological advancement.

**Data Source**

The research relies on the NBER-CES Manufacturing Industry Database (version nberces5818v1). This dataset acts as a comprehensive census of US manufacturing industries at a 6-digit NAICS code resolution, covering the timeframe from 1958 to 2000 to isolate the crisis impact from later macroeconomic shocks (like the 2008 crash).

**Methodology**

The analysis uses advanced econometrics in Python to establish causal inference:

Dynamic Difference-in-Differences (DiD): Three core OLS regression models evaluate the divergence between the treatment group (High-Energy) and the control group (Low-Energy) before and after 1973.

Two-Way Fixed Effects: The models incorporate C(year) (Year Fixed Effects) and C(naics) (Industry Fixed Effects) to control for macroeconomic shocks and unobserved, constant structural characteristics of the industries.

Propensity Score Matching (PSM): To handle potential selection bias, the study uses a dynamic, year-by-year Nearest Neighbor matching algorithm with logistic regression. This ensures an "apples-to-apples" comparison by matching industries with similar base characteristics.

**Repository Structure**

The repository is structured to separate raw data, documentation, analysis scripts, and visual outputs:

data/: Contains the NBER-CES dataset files used for the analysis.

notebooks/: Houses the Jupyter Notebooks containing the data cleaning, variable definitions, PSM algorithm, and the Dynamic DiD regression models.

figures/: Stores the event study plots, dynamic trend charts, and descriptive statistics graphs generated by the code.

**Technologies**

Python (Data processing, econometric modeling, PSM, and visualization), Jupyter Notebook.
